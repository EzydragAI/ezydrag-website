"Human in the loop" is not a button. Stop shipping it like one…

Santanu Mondal, Director of Design9 Nov 2026 · 2 min read
TL;DR

An "Approve?" button with no context trains people to click yes. Design the approval moment like a product screen, not a pop-up.

Let's start with a small experiment

Think about the last cookie banner you saw. Did you read it? No. You clicked the big button. Everyone does.

Now imagine that banner decides whether an email goes to your biggest customer. Scary, na?

That's what most "human in the loop" screens look like today. One line. Two buttons. And after the fifth one, your reviewer is on autopilot.

If the reviewer can't tell what happens next, the approval is decoration.

What the research says

In July, Paul Bryant wrote a piece called "Human-in-the-Loop Is Not a Button." His point is simple: approval is a control problem, not a UI sticker. The real question is "whether the approval path is scoped, timed, routed, evidenced, and accountable."

He also gives a timing rule I love: ask right before the side effect. That's the moment the reviewer can see the tool, the target, the payload and the impact.

Look around you

Ever noticed how a payment app shows the name, the amount and the account before you hit Pay? That screen exists because the moment just before an irreversible action is when people actually pay attention.

In August, Margaret Mitchell, Avijit Ghosh and Samir Passi went further in a position paper titled "AI Agents Push Humans Out of the Loop." They argue that today's agent design makes real oversight harder, and that supporting the overseer should matter as much as the agent's capability.

Let's tear down a good approval card

Here's the anatomy. Four things, one glance:

  1. What happens. "Send this email to Priya on Tuesday."
  2. Why. "Score 82. Visited pricing twice."
  3. How far the yes goes. This send only. Not every send, forever.
  4. What if nobody answers. It waits. It never "continues anyway".
Plot twist

A user study cited in Marmelab's September harness report found that writing permission rules up front blocked 20.1 points fewer bad actions than approving each action. Per-action approval works, if you design it well.

What I'd tell a founder

Sketch the approval card before you build the agent. If you can't fill in the "why" line, the agent isn't ready yet. That's not a design problem. That's a product problem wearing a design costume.

How we design it at Ezydrag

Our approval cards always show the action, the reason and the name that goes into the log. You can see one in the trust section of this site. It's boring on purpose. Boring is easy to trust.
